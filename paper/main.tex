\title{The Price of De-correlation: Quantifying the Agreement--Cost Trade-off

\begin{abstract}
Large language model (LLM) ensembles are increasingly used as decision-making
committees under the assumption that aggregating multiple agents improves
reliability. However, ensembles composed of repeated instances of the same model
may produce correlated judgments and create an appearance of consensus without
genuine independence. This study quantifies the relationship between
cross-provider model heterogeneity, ensemble agreement, and inference cost. In a
54{,}000-call primary experiment, we evaluate three five-agent ensemble
configurations with increasing provider diversity on a directional
financial-analysis task covering 100 equities across 12 dates. Within the evaluated configurations, greater cross-provider
heterogeneity is associated with significantly lower within-ensemble agreement,
with a primary reduction of $\Delta\kappa=0.336$ (95\% CI: $0.304$--$0.369$), but increases
inference cost by approximately $4.4\times$. Because agreement does not by itself
measure dependence between errors, we additionally estimate the correlation
between agents' binary error indicators. Error correlation decreases by
$\Delta\phi = 0.454$ (95\% CI: $0.376$ to $0.534$), while mean per-agent accuracy
remains essentially unchanged. This indicates that the observed change reflects
lower error dependence rather than differences in average correctness.
The contrast remains substantial under approximate capability
matching, although that control does not isolate provider from other
model-specific characteristics.
We also introduce an independent-draw measurement protocol designed to prevent
shared sampling conditions from inflating estimated agreement. A pre-registered
replication on a substantially different task involving 537 expert
clinical-decision items reproduces the directional pattern. Agreement decreases by
$\Delta\kappa = 0.072$ (95\% CI: $0.040$ to $0.104$), while error correlation
decreases by $\Delta\phi = 0.393$ (95\% CI: $0.325$ to $0.459$). Although
heterogeneity does not produce a statistically reliable improvement in average
directional accuracy, exploratory analysis shows that homogeneous ensembles
generate unanimous incorrect decisions at $1.84$ times the rate of fully
heterogeneous ensembles (95\% CI: $1.42$ to $2.52$). Taken together, the results
show that heterogeneous model composition can reduce error dependence without
guaranteeing greater predictive accuracy, while imposing a measurable inference
cost premium.
\end{abstract}

Ensemble learning, error correlation, large language models, model heterogeneity,
multi-agent systems.

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}
\PARstart{M}{ulti-agent} large language model systems increasingly aggregate multiple agents
through voting, debate, or other consensus
mechanisms~\cite{du2023debate,wang2024moa}. These approaches are commonly
motivated by the wisdom-of-crowds principle, under which combining multiple
judgments can reduce individual errors. Their effectiveness, however, depends on
the extent to which the participating agents produce independent errors. An
ensemble comprising repeated instances of the same model may exhibit substantial
output correlation, thereby providing less effective diversity than its number
of agents suggests. Cross-provider model heterogeneity offers a potential
remedy, but it also introduces additional inference cost, and its ability to
reduce correlated behavior has not been established quantitatively. Recent
evidence that LLMs may exhibit correlated errors even across providers and
architectures further raises concerns about algorithmic
monoculture~\cite{kim2025correlated}.

Existing research supports two competing expectations regarding cross-provider
heterogeneity. Under the diversity hypothesis, models developed by different
providers, using distinct training procedures, datasets, and architectures,
should generate less-correlated outputs and therefore contribute more diverse
judgments. In contrast, the algorithmic-monoculture
hypothesis~\cite{kim2025correlated} suggests that common training data,
objectives, and evaluation practices may cause substantial behavioral similarity
even across model families. These perspectives imply materially different
agreement--cost relationships. If provider identity contributes little
additional diversity, increasing heterogeneity will raise inference cost without
meaningfully reducing agreement. Conversely, a substantial reduction in
agreement would indicate that cross-provider diversity is measurable and can be
evaluated against its associated cost. This study tests these alternatives
empirically.

Despite the growing use of heterogeneous LLM ensembles, three related issues
remain insufficiently examined. First, few studies directly quantify the extent
to which cross-provider heterogeneity is associated with lower within-ensemble
agreement. Second,
the corresponding inference-cost premium is rarely incorporated into evaluations
of ensemble design. Third, agreement estimates may be distorted when supposedly
independent agents share random seeds, sampling trajectories, cached outputs, or
other elements of the generation context. Consequently, existing evaluations may
conflate genuine model similarity with artifacts of the measurement procedure.
We address these limitations by jointly estimating the reduction in ensemble
agreement, the associated inference cost, and the effect of independent-draw
measurement controls.

This distinction is practically important because LLM ensembles are increasingly
considered for financial analysis and other consequential decision-support
applications in which multiple agents are expected to provide independent second
opinions. When agents exhibit highly correlated behavior, repeated calls may
increase computational expenditure while producing an overstated impression of
evidential consensus. Practitioners therefore require evidence on both the
degree of independence obtained through model heterogeneity and the cost
required to obtain it. By estimating these quantities jointly, this study
provides an empirical basis for selecting ensemble configurations according to
the reliability and cost requirements of a given application.

\paragraph{Contributions}
This paper makes the following contributions.
\begin{enumerate}
\item \emph{Pre-registered empirical measurement.} We conduct a pre-registered
empirical study comprising 54{,}000 model calls to quantify the association
between cross-provider heterogeneity and within-ensemble agreement. Agreement decreases
substantially and monotonically as provider diversity increases, with a primary
contrast of $\Delta\kappa=0.336$ and a 95\% confidence interval of
$[0.304, 0.369]$.
\item \emph{An empirical agreement--cost frontier.} We quantify the
inference-cost premium associated with cross-provider diversity. Moving from the
homogeneous to the fully heterogeneous configuration reduces agreement at an
approximately $4.4\times$ increase in inference cost.
\item \emph{A reproducible independent-draw measurement protocol.} We separate
genuine model agreement from agreement induced by shared sampling conditions. In
a controlled pilot, shared per-run seeding inflated the estimated agreement
contrast by approximately fourfold, whereas unique per-agent seeds removed the
artifact. To support replication, we provide a frozen, hash-verified dataset,
fixed experimental configurations, and a deterministic seeded analysis pipeline
that regenerates the reported figures and tables.
\item \emph{An exploratory analysis of consensus structure.} Although
heterogeneity does not produce a statistically reliable improvement in average
directional accuracy, homogeneous ensembles generate unanimous incorrect
directional decisions at $1.84\times$ the rate of fully heterogeneous ensembles,
motivating confirmatory evaluation of collective-error risk.
\end{enumerate}
We distinguish reduction in ensemble agreement from improvement in predictive
accuracy. Cross-provider heterogeneity substantially increases observed
behavioral diversity, but it does not reliably improve average correctness in
the evaluated directional-finance task. Thus, model diversity should be
evaluated according to whether the additional diversity improves the downstream
objective rather than being treated as beneficial by default.

\section{Related Work}
\subsection{LLM ensembles and mixture-of-agents}
Aggregating multiple LLM outputs through voting, debate, or layered
mixture-of-agents architectures has been investigated as a means of improving
response quality and reliability~\cite{du2023debate,wang2024moa,chen2025survey}.
However, prior studies indicate that naive aggregation may provide only limited
gains over the strongest individual model and may amplify systematic errors when
correlated weaker models outvote a stronger model~\cite{kim2025correlated}.
Related scaling studies also report diminishing or negative returns from
repeatedly adding homogeneous agents, whereas more diverse ensembles can provide
more sustained improvements~\cite{li2024moreagents}.

These findings have motivated diversity-aware methods such as the
focal-diversity pruning strategy used in LLM-TOPLA~\cite{tekin2024topla}, the
fingerprint-based ensemble selection of DFPE~\cite{dfpe2025}, and heterogeneous
consensus frameworks such as Council Mode~\cite{council2026}. Such approaches
generally evaluate diversity through downstream performance. In contrast, the
present study treats within-ensemble agreement as the primary quantity of
interest and directly measures how agreement, statistical uncertainty, and
inference cost vary across configurations with differing cross-provider
heterogeneity.

\subsection{Correlated errors and algorithmic monoculture}
Kim \textit{et al.}~\cite{kim2025correlated} document substantial error
correlation across a large set of LLMs, including models developed by different
providers, and relate this dependence to the systemic risk of algorithmic
monoculture. Their analysis concerns error correlation---that is, the tendency
of models to fail on the same instances---whereas the present study examines
output agreement across all decisions, regardless of whether those decisions are
correct.

These quantities are related but not equivalent. Lower output agreement may
indicate greater behavioral diversity, but it does not necessarily imply
less-correlated errors or improved predictive performance. In the
vision-language setting, Bugaud~\cite{bugaud2026} similarly reports that models
from the same architectural family exhibit correlated errors that reduce the
effective number of independent voters and shows that family-aware aggregation
can improve ensemble performance.

The present work extends this line of inquiry to text-based LLM committees by
measuring provider-level agreement, comparing same-provider and cross-provider
agent pairs, and incorporating the inference-cost trade-off associated with
model heterogeneity.

\subsection{Inter-rater agreement and diversity metrics}
Fleiss' $\kappa$~\cite{fleiss1971} is a chance-corrected agreement statistic
designed for categorical ratings produced by multiple raters. We apply it by
treating the five LLM agents as interchangeable raters, each
$(\mathrm{ticker},\mathrm{date})$ pair as a rated subject, and BUY, HOLD, and
SELL as nominal categories.

Unlike diversity measures used primarily to select, weight, or prune ensemble
members~\cite{tekin2024topla}, our objective is to estimate agreement itself and
determine how it varies with provider heterogeneity and inference cost. Because
Fleiss' $\kappa$ can be sensitive to category prevalence, we additionally assess
the robustness of the observed configuration ordering using Krippendorff's
$\alpha$~\cite{krippendorff2019} and Gwet's AC1~\cite{gwet2008}. Statistical
uncertainty is estimated through cluster-bootstrap resampling at the equity
level~\cite{efron1993}.

\subsection{Reproducibility and pre-registration}
Pre-registration and frozen, hash-verified datasets remain uncommon in empirical
LLM research. We adopt a confirmatory design---specifying the analysis plan in
advance, collecting data once, and re-analyzing deterministically---to limit
researcher degrees of freedom, and we release the resulting artifacts. The
freezing, seeding, and reproducibility procedures are described in detail in
Section~\ref{sec:prereg}.

\section{Problem Formulation and Metrics}
\subsection{Ensemble configurations}
Each ensemble contains five agents that independently respond to the same prompt
through separate application programming interface (API) calls. We designate OpenAI as the primary provider and
define the heterogeneity level of configuration $c$ as the number of agents
drawn from a non-primary provider:
\begin{equation}
h_c=\sum_{i=1}^{5}\mathbb{I}\left(p_i\neq p_{\mathrm{primary}}\right),
\end{equation}
where $p_i$ denotes the provider of agent $i$. We evaluate three
configurations:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{HOM} ($h{=}0$): five agents using the primary-provider model;
\item \textbf{HET-LITE} ($h{=}1$): four primary-provider agents and one Google
agent;
\item \textbf{HET} ($h{=}3$): two primary-provider agents, two Google agents,
and one xAI agent.
\end{itemize}
The exact model identifiers, provider roles, and token prices used during data
collection are reported in Table~\ref{tab:models}.

\subsection{Agreement statistic}
For each configuration $c$, experimental run $r$, and evaluation cell
$s=(\mathrm{ticker},\mathrm{date})$, the five agents return one categorical
direction from $\mathcal{Y}=\{\mathrm{BUY},\mathrm{HOLD},\mathrm{SELL}\}$. We
treat agents as raters and evaluation cells as rated subjects. Fleiss' $\kappa$
is computed separately within each run, producing the run-level agreement
statistic $\kappa_{c,r}$. Configuration-level agreement is then defined as the
mean across the $R=3$ runs:
\begin{equation}
\kappa_c=\frac{1}{R}\sum_{r=1}^{R}\kappa_{c,r}.
\end{equation}
The pre-registered primary endpoint is the reduction in agreement between the
homogeneous and fully heterogeneous configurations:
\begin{equation}
\Delta\kappa_{\mathrm{HOM-HET}}=\kappa_{\mathrm{HOM}}-\kappa_{\mathrm{HET}}.
\label{eq:primary}
\end{equation}
% Set inline, these two subscripted contrasts are wider than the column: TeX held the relation
% sign on the line and overran the right margin by 13.76pt, visibly. Displayed and stacked they
% fit, and they read better beside the primary contrast in (3) directly above. Unnumbered, so no

The pre-registered secondary contrasts are
\[
\begin{aligned}
\Delta\kappa_{\mathrm{HOM-HET\text{-}LITE}}&=\kappa_{\mathrm{HOM}}-\kappa_{\mathrm{HET\text{-}LITE}},\\
\Delta\kappa_{\mathrm{HET\text{-}LITE-HET}}&=\kappa_{\mathrm{HET\text{-}LITE}}-\kappa_{\mathrm{HET}}.
\end{aligned}
\]
Positive values indicate that configurations with greater cross-provider
heterogeneity show lower within-ensemble agreement.

\subsection{Independent-draw estimand}
\label{subsec:estimand}
\label{sec:estimand}
The estimand of interest is agreement among independently sampled agent
responses. Accordingly, every agent response is generated through a separate,
stateless API request, without shared conversation state, response caching, or
multi-sample batching, so that no two agents share a sampling trajectory through
the request structure itself. Each request is additionally assigned a unique,
reproducible seed:
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{seed}_{r,c,s,i}=\mathrm{SHA\text{-}256}(\mathrm{master},r,c,\mathrm{ticker},\mathrm{date},i)\bmod 2^{31},
\end{equation}
where $i$ identifies the agent position within the ensemble. Including the
agent identifier gives repeated instances of the same model distinct seeds---and
therefore distinct sampling trajectories on endpoints that honor the seed
parameter---while keeping those calls reproducible. All three provider endpoints
used in this study accept a seed parameter, and the unique per-agent seed is
transmitted on every call, masked to a positive 32-bit integer as some endpoints
require; the model registry and supplementary manifest record the seed sent with
each call. Because provider seed support is best-effort rather than a
determinism guarantee, reproducibility ultimately rests on the archived raw
responses (Section~\ref{sec:prereg}) in addition to re-executable seeding.

As a diagnostic for residual within-run dependence, we compare agreement among
the five draws generated within a run with agreement estimated from
independently generated draws across runs. For the homogeneous configuration,
these quantities should be approximately equal when all five agents are sampled
independently. A materially positive within-run minus cross-run gap would
indicate that shared seeds or another common sampling condition is artificially
increasing measured agreement. For heterogeneous configurations, the cross-run
calculation also introduces additional same-model replicate pairs and is
therefore interpreted as a secondary consistency check rather than a direct
independence test.

\subsection{Uncertainty quantification}
\label{subsec:uncertainty}
Uncertainty in the agreement contrasts is estimated using a cluster bootstrap
with equities as the resampling unit~\cite{efron1993}. Clustering at the equity
level preserves the dependence among repeated analysis dates for the same
security while allowing variation across securities to determine the confidence
interval. For each of 2{,}000 bootstrap replications, equities are sampled with
replacement, all associated dates and agent responses are retained, and the
configuration-level agreement statistics and contrasts are recomputed. We use
the percentile method with a fixed random-number-generator seed of 42 to
construct 95\% confidence intervals (CIs). Under the pre-registered decision
rule, the
primary hypothesis is considered supported when
$\Delta\kappa_{\mathrm{HOM-HET}}>0$ and its 95\% confidence interval excludes
zero.

Equity is the pre-registered resampling unit because repeated observations are
nested within securities. Each equity is evaluated on 12 analysis dates, and
observations for the same security share company-specific context. Analysis dates
constitute a second crossed source of dependence because all securities evaluated
on a given date share common market conditions.

Because equity and analysis date are crossed rather than nested, we additionally
assess uncertainty under a two-way resampling procedure. This follows the
crossed-resampling principle of the pigeonhole
bootstrap~\cite{owen2007pigeonhole}, in which the two margins are resampled
separately, and is motivated by the broader multiway-inference literature for
non-nested clustering dimensions~\cite{cameron2011multiway}; it is not an
implementation of a multiway variance estimator. We therefore assess the primary
contrast under three resampling schemes: equity clustering over 100 equities,
date clustering over 12 dates, and a two-way procedure in which equities and
dates are resampled independently, with multiplicity preserved on both margins,
and their sampled intersections retained. Results appear in Table~\ref{tab:clustering}. The
point estimate is unchanged across schemes. The two-way interval is the widest, as
expected when both dependence structures are admitted, and it continues to exclude
zero.

Because the date dimension contains only 12 clusters, inference based on that
margin may be imprecise. The pre-registered equity-clustered interval therefore
remains the primary analysis, while the date-clustered and two-way results are
reported as sensitivity analyses.

\section{Experimental Design}
\subsection{Task and grid}
For each evaluation cell $s=(j,t)$, where $j$ denotes an equity and $t$ denotes
an analysis date, every agent receives the same dated information set and
independently returns a response conforming to the following JavaScript Object
Notation (JSON) schema:
\begin{lstlisting}
{
  "direction":  "BUY | HOLD | SELL",
  "conviction": <integer 1-5>,
  "rationale":  "<free-text explanation>"
}
\end{lstlisting}
The \texttt{direction} field constitutes the categorical output used in the
primary agreement analysis. The \texttt{conviction} field is an integer from 1
to 5, and the \texttt{conviction} and \texttt{rationale} fields are retained for
secondary and exploratory analyses. The complete prompt template and response
schema are frozen and included in the reproducibility package.

Each response is parsed as strict JSON against this schema. A response that
fails to parse, omits a required field, or returns a direction outside
$\{\mathrm{BUY},\mathrm{HOLD},\mathrm{SELL}\}$ is treated as invalid and
reissued with the same per-agent seed, up to a pre-registered maximum of five
retries, with every attempt logged. Calls interrupted by provider daily-quota
limits were deferred and re-issued after the quota reset under the same seeds. A
cell that remained invalid after the retry budget would have been handled under
the pre-registered exclusion rule; in the confirmatory dataset, all 54{,}000
grid cells ultimately returned schema-valid responses, so no cells were
excluded. The complete attempt history, including failed and deferred calls, is
preserved in the released log.

The experiment covers 100 large-cap equities distributed across all 11 Global
Industry Classification Standard sectors and 12 analysis dates in 2026. For
each equity-date pair, responses are generated under three ensemble
configurations, three independent runs, and five agents per ensemble. The
complete design therefore contains
$100\times 12\times 3\times 3\times 5=54{,}000$ individual model calls.

The dates are selected to reduce the possibility that model responses rely on
memorized post-event information and to ensure that the 20-trading-day
forward-return window used in the secondary accuracy analysis has fully elapsed.
They are also chosen to span distinct 2026 market conditions. The equity
universe, sector allocation, inclusion criteria, and complete ticker list are
reported in the supplementary materials.

\subsection{No-lookahead context}
Each agent receives a price-derived information set constructed solely from
observations available through the trading day immediately preceding the
corresponding analysis date. For every equity-date cell, the context contains
the trailing one-week return, the trailing one-month return, the trailing
three-month return, the 21-trading-day realized volatility, and the security's
position within its trailing 63-trading-day price range. No observations from
the analysis date or any subsequent date are included in the agent prompt. The
same information set is supplied to all agents evaluating a given cell, ensuring
that differences in responses arise from model behavior rather than differences
in the available inputs.

For exact replication, the return horizons are defined in trading days. For
example, the range-position feature is computed as
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{RangePos}_{j,t}=\frac{P_{j,t-1}-\min_{\tau\in W_{63}}P_{j,\tau}}
{\max_{\tau\in W_{63}}P_{j,\tau}-\min_{\tau\in W_{63}}P_{j,\tau}},
\end{equation}
where $W_{63}$ denotes the preceding 63-trading-day window. End-of-day prices
are obtained from a single market-data vendor (Financial Modeling Prep), fetched
once, and frozen in the released cache; realized volatility is computed over the
trailing 21-trading-day window, and the treatment of missing observations is
specified in the supplementary materials. Data provenance and the exact
information supplied to each model call are recorded in the experimental logs.

\subsection{Models}
All model calls are executed using a sampling temperature of 0.7. Exact provider
and model identifiers are fixed for the duration of data collection and reported
in Table~\ref{tab:models}, together with their roles in each ensemble
configuration and the input- and output-token prices applicable during the
experiment. Each agent response is generated through a separate API request
under the independent-sampling protocol described in
Section~\ref{sec:estimand}. Apart from the model and provider assignments
defined by the ensemble configuration, agents evaluating the same cell receive
identical prompts and inference settings.

Because proprietary model endpoints may be modified or replaced by their
providers, the archived call-level dataset constitutes the empirical record of
the experiment. The conclusions therefore apply to the responses generated by
the specified endpoints during the reported collection period rather than to
future versions carrying similar product names. To support replication and
auditability, the experimental log records the model identifier, provider,
timestamp, seed, prompt hash, token usage, cost, and raw response for every
call.

\subsection{Pre-registration, freezing, and reproducibility}
\label{sec:prereg}
Empirical evaluations of LLM systems involve several design choices, including
model selection, prompt construction, sampling parameters, evaluation endpoints,
exclusion rules, and statistical procedures. Revising these choices after
observing the results can increase researcher degrees of freedom and make
replication more difficult. We therefore adopt a confirmatory workflow that
separates pre-specified analyses from subsequent exploratory investigations.

Before confirmatory data collection, we froze the hypotheses, ensemble
configurations, prompt template, experimental grid, random-seed construction,
primary and secondary endpoints, uncertainty-estimation procedure, and decision
rule. The resulting pre-registration was assigned a SHA-256 hash to establish
that the design preceded the collection of the confirmatory dataset. The released
package contains timestamped cryptographic freeze receipts establishing that the
pre-registered design preceded data collection. Each receipt records the UTC
freeze time, the commit that carried the frozen artifacts, and the SHA-256 of the
configuration, agent and analysis code, prompt template, pre-registration, grid,
and model manifest; the cross-domain replication has its own receipt, recorded
before any API call for that arm. Raw outputs
were collected once and stored in an immutable, read-only, hash-verified
dataset. The analysis pipeline reads only the frozen dataset and experimental
configuration, and all stochastic procedures, including bootstrap resampling,
use fixed seeds. The pipeline regenerates the reported tables and figures
deterministically.

Analyses not included in the original pre-registration are identified explicitly
as exploratory or as documented amendments. This separation prevents post hoc
results from being presented as confirmatory evidence and allows the primary
findings to be evaluated against the design specified before data collection.
The frozen dataset, pre-registration materials, orchestration configuration, and
analysis code are included in the accompanying reproducibility package.

\subsection{Collection provenance and retry accounting}
\label{subsec:provenance}
The confirmatory grid contains 54{,}000 planned responses, all of which
ultimately returned valid outputs. Completing the grid required 94{,}566 API
requests. Of the 40{,}566 unsuccessful requests, 40{,}544 were provider
rate-limit or quota responses and 22 were transport or schema failures. Because
rate-limit and quota responses returned no model completion, retrying them did
not select observations based on generated content.

Every request attempt is retained in the released log. Request settings were
fixed across configurations, including temperature 0.7, per-agent seed
construction, a 256-token completion limit, and strict JSON output. The released
reproducibility materials contain the prompts, prompt hashes, model identifiers,
prices, experimental configuration, and preregistration records.

\section{Results}
The analysis includes the complete planned set of 54{,}000 model calls across
100 equities, 12 analysis dates, three ensemble configurations, three
independent runs, and five agents per ensemble. Unless otherwise stated,
agreement statistics are computed separately within each run and then averaged
across runs.

\subsection{Heterogeneity and ensemble agreement (primary)}
\label{subsec:primary}
Within-ensemble agreement decreases monotonically as cross-provider
heterogeneity increases. Fleiss' $\kappa$ is 0.552 for HOM, 0.302 for HET-LITE,
and 0.215 for HET, as reported in Table~\ref{tab:frontier} and illustrated in
Fig.~\ref{fig:frontier}. Using the unrounded configuration-level estimates, the
pre-registered primary contrast is
\[
\Delta\kappa_{\mathrm{HOM-HET}}=\kappa_{\mathrm{HOM}}-\kappa_{\mathrm{HET}}=0.336,
\]
with a 95\% cluster-bootstrap confidence interval of $[0.304, 0.369]$. Because
the confidence interval excludes zero, the pre-registered primary hypothesis
that greater cross-provider heterogeneity is associated with lower
within-ensemble agreement is supported. In Landis--Koch~\cite{landis1977} terms, homogeneous ensembles reach
``moderate--substantial'' internal agreement while fully heterogeneous ones fall
to ``fair.'' Both pre-registered secondary contrasts are also positive, with
confidence intervals excluding zero (Table~\ref{tab:endpoints}); under the
two-way scheme of Section~\ref{subsec:uncertainty} they remain so, at
$[0.208,\,0.290]$ and $[0.045,\,0.123]$ respectively. Agreement
therefore decreases at each observed increase in heterogeneity, from HOM to
HET-LITE and from HET-LITE to HET. Across the evaluated
configurations the ordering is monotonic: agreement is lower at each higher level
of cross-provider heterogeneity in this task.

\subsection{Robustness of the agreement statistic}
The marginal distribution of BUY, HOLD, and SELL responses changes across
ensemble configurations. In particular, the proportion of HOLD responses
decreases from 0.71 in HOM to 0.56 in HET. Because Fleiss' $\kappa$ can be
sensitive to category prevalence (the ``$\kappa$ paradox''), this shift could
affect its absolute value independently of changes in the underlying level of
agreement. We therefore recompute agreement using Krippendorff's
$\alpha$~\cite{krippendorff2019} for nominal outcomes and Gwet's
AC1~\cite{gwet2008}. All three statistics produce the same monotonic
configuration ordering: HOM $>$ HET-LITE $>$ HET (Table~\ref{tab:stats}).
Krippendorff's $\alpha$ closely tracks Fleiss' $\kappa$, while Gwet's AC1
produces higher absolute values but preserves both the ordering and the
substantial HOM-to-HET reduction. The primary conclusion is therefore robust to
alternative treatments of chance agreement and is not attributable solely to
changes in the category marginals.

\subsection{Agreement, error correlation, and decorrelation}
\label{subsec:errcorr}
Agreement and error dependence capture different properties of an ensemble. We
therefore distinguish five concepts used throughout the analysis:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \emph{Output agreement}: the extent to which agents return the same
categorical label. This is measured by Fleiss' $\kappa$ and constitutes the
primary agreement endpoint.
\item \emph{Error correlation}: dependence between agents' mistakes. This is
measured using $\phi$ computed from binary error indicators.
\item \emph{Sampling independence}: whether repeated responses share elements of
the sampling process, such as a random seed or decoding trajectory.
\item \emph{Model diversity}: differences in model weights, architecture,
training data, or related model characteristics.
\item \emph{Provider diversity}: whether agents are supplied by different
organizations. The ensemble configurations vary provider composition, although
provider remains bundled with other model-specific characteristics.
\end{enumerate}

In this study, decorrelation denotes a reduction in error correlation, measured
by $\phi$. The agreement measure $\kappa$ captures a related but distinct change
in categorical consensus. Accordingly, the ``price of de-correlation'' in the
title refers to the additional inference cost associated with ensemble
configurations that exhibit lower error dependence.

Pairwise dependence between binary error indicators is a standard
characterization of classifier-ensemble diversity, and that literature also shows
that diversity and ensemble accuracy are related but not
interchangeable~\cite{kuncheva2003diversity}. We therefore complement the
categorical agreement statistic $\kappa$ with a $\phi$ correlation computed from
paired error indicators. Within each evaluation cell, that is each
(configuration, equity, date, run)
combination, we form for every unordered pair of agent positions the pair of
binary error indicators defined against the realized forward-return sign. These
paired indicators are pooled over all cells and all agent-position pairs within a
configuration, and $\phi$ is the Pearson correlation of that pooled set, so one
$\phi$ is reported per configuration rather than one per agent pair. Error
correlation decreases from $\phi = 0.984$ for HOM to $0.742$ for HET-LITE and
$0.530$ for HET, giving $\Delta\phi_{\mathrm{HOM-HET}} = 0.454$
(95\% CI $[0.376,\,0.534]$). Under the two-way scheme that also admits date
dependence, the interval widens to $[0.328,\,0.586]$ and still excludes zero.

Because treating HOLD as an abstention changes the scoreable subset, we repeat
the analysis with HOLD counted as an error. The corresponding values are $0.586$,
$0.359$, and $0.277$, with $\Delta\phi_{\mathrm{HOM-HET}} = 0.309$
(95\% CI $[0.262,\,0.354]$; two-way $[0.227,\,0.387]$). The ordering is preserved
under both treatments of HOLD, and under both resampling schemes.

Mean per-agent error rates are similar across configurations, indicating that the
decline in $\phi$ reflects lower error dependence rather than differences in
average correctness. The reduction is partial rather than complete, as
heterogeneous agents continue to make correlated errors.

\subsection{The agreement--cost frontier}
\label{subsec:frontier}
The reduction in agreement is accompanied by higher inference cost. Cost per
ensemble decision is calculated from metered prompt and completion tokens. For
each call, provider-reported token counts are priced using the per-million token
rates reported in Table~\ref{tab:models}. Costs are summed across the five agents
within a cell and then averaged across cells within each configuration.

Prices are fixed at those applicable during data collection, so differences across
configurations reflect token usage and model composition rather than later price
changes. No discount, batching, or caching adjustment is applied. Failed requests
that return no billable completion are excluded. The observed
cost per five-agent ensemble decision increases from approximately
$\$6.5\times 10^{-4}$ for HOM to $\$1.5\times 10^{-3}$ for HET-LITE and
$\$2.8\times 10^{-3}$ for HET. Moving from the homogeneous to the fully
heterogeneous configuration therefore increases inference cost by approximately
$4.4\times$ (Table~\ref{tab:frontier}). Fig.~\ref{fig:frontier} presents
within-ensemble agreement against inference cost for the three configurations.
The resulting frontier quantifies the additional expenditure associated with
lower agreement and allows ensemble configurations to be compared jointly on
behavioral diversity and cost. The frontier does not, by itself, establish that
the additional disagreement improves downstream predictive performance.

\subsection{Shared-seed dependence and measurement validation}
\label{sec:protocol}
\paragraph{Exactly what was shared}
In the initial pilot implementation, the random seed was derived at the
ensemble-run level rather than at the individual-agent level. Consequently, all
five same-model agents evaluating a given cell received the same prompt and
seed. Under this configuration, the calls produced near-verbatim responses,
indicating that they did not function as independent draws for the purpose of
measuring homogeneous-model agreement. The confirmatory implementation instead
assigns every agent a unique, reproducible seed determined by its run,
configuration, equity, date, and agent position (Fig.~\ref{fig:seed}). The
prompt, model, temperature, and experimental grid remain unchanged. This
modification preserves reproducibility while allowing repeated instances of the
same model to follow distinct sampling trajectories.

\begin{figure}[!t]
\centering
\begin{lstlisting}
# Before (artifact): one seed shared by
# all agents in a run
seed = f(master, run)
for agent in 1..5:
    call(model, prompt, seed)  # identical draw

# After (fix): distinct per-agent seed
seed = SHA256(master, run, config,
              ticker, date, agent) & 0x7FFFFFFF
call(model, prompt, seed)  # distinct seed
\end{lstlisting}

same-model agents shared a sampling path. The study assigns a distinct
reproducible seed to each agent, removing the shared-seed artifact observed in
the pilot. As Section~\ref{subsec:estimand} notes, this does not establish
statistical independence of the responses.}
\label{fig:seed}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{Ruling out adjacent explanations}
The pilot duplication was not attributable to request batching, because every
agent response was generated through a separate API request. Prompt caching was
also disabled during collection. Within the controlled pilot setup, the
disappearance of near-duplicate behavior after introducing per-agent seeds is
therefore consistent with shared-seed dependence as the source of the inflated
agreement.

\paragraph{Diagnostic verification}
We evaluate residual within-run dependence by comparing within-run and cross-run
agreement (Table~\ref{tab:nondet}). After adopting unique per-agent seeds, the
HOM within-minus-cross difference is approximately 0.001, indicating that
agreement among the five agents within a run is nearly identical to agreement
obtained from separately generated draws across runs. In the controlled pilot,
the estimated primary contrast was 0.485 under shared per-run seeding and 0.113
under independent per-agent seeding; shared seeding therefore overstated the
estimated agreement contrast by approximately fourfold
(Fig.~\ref{fig:trap}). The pilot grid comprised 8 equities $\times$ 2 analysis
dates $\times$ 2 configurations (HOM and HET) $\times$ 3 runs $\times$ 5
agents, i.e.\ 480 calls per seeding arm---far smaller than the confirmatory
grid---so the pilot contrasts are point estimates whose role is diagnostic,
demonstrating the artifact and its removal, rather than confirmatory. This
factor is specific to the pilot configuration and should not be interpreted as
a universal inflation constant for all LLM ensemble studies. The heterogeneous configurations produce within-minus-cross differences
of $-0.048$ and $-0.074$. These differences arise because cross-run pooling
introduces additional repeated draws from the same underlying models, increasing
pooled agreement relative to the single-draw within-run calculation. The primary
endpoint uses the within-run estimate and is therefore not increased by this
pooling effect.

\paragraph{Sensitivity: agreement by pair type}
We additionally separate pairwise agreement according to whether two agents use
the same model. Same-model agreement is $0.800$ in HOM, $0.794$ in HET-LITE, and
$0.862$ in HET, while different-model agreement is $0.455$ in HET-LITE and
$0.490$ in HET.

The reduction in ensemble agreement is therefore associated with the introduction
of different-model pairs rather than a systematic decline in agreement among
repeated instances of the same model. This analysis is a sensitivity check on the
frozen dataset and does not independently verify provider seed behavior.

\paragraph{What unique seeds do and do not establish}
Assigning a distinct seed to each agent removes the specific shared-seed artifact
observed in the pilot. It does not establish statistical independence of the
resulting responses.

Seed support in commercial inference systems is best-effort rather than a
guarantee of deterministic execution. Distinct per-agent seeds are therefore
treated as a control against one demonstrated dependence mechanism, not as proof
of independence.

\subsection{Provider-pair agreement}
The pairwise directional-agreement matrix in Fig.~\ref{fig:provider} provides
additional evidence on where the reduction in ensemble agreement comes from.
Same-provider agreement is 0.92 for Google agent pairs and 0.80 for OpenAI agent
pairs, whereas cross-provider agreement ranges from 0.43 to 0.63. Agents
supplied by the same provider therefore agree more frequently than agents
supplied by different providers under identical prompt and information
conditions.

This comparison should be interpreted as a configuration-specific observational
finding rather than as an isolated provider effect. In the present design,
provider is not experimentally separable from model identity, parameter count,
training corpus, architecture, or alignment procedure because each provider
contributes one model. The observed contrast therefore reflects substitution among
the specific model and provider combinations evaluated here. The term ``provider''
should be understood as shorthand for that bundle of characteristics rather than
as an independently identified causal factor.

Section~\ref{subsec:crossdomain} provides an additional capability-matched
comparison on a second domain, in which one same-provider pair and one
cross-provider pair have nearly identical member accuracy. The
provider-associated ordering persists under that comparison, but provider remains
inseparable from other model-specific characteristics. A design that varied model
identity within provider and provider within capability tier would be required to
separate these effects further.

\subsection{Secondary directional-accuracy analysis}
As a pre-declared secondary analysis, we compare each ensemble's majority-vote
direction with the sign of the realized 20-trading-day forward return. HOLD
decisions are treated as abstentions and excluded from the corresponding
hit-rate calculation. The estimated directional hit rates are 0.438 for HOM,
0.463 for HET-LITE, and 0.488 for HET (Table~\ref{tab:acc}). The associated
Wilson confidence intervals~\cite{wilson1927} are wide and overlapping.
Moreover, the experiment was powered for the agreement endpoint rather than for
differences in directional accuracy. The estimates therefore do not provide
sufficient evidence that cross-provider heterogeneity improves average
directional accuracy. The numerical upward trend is reported descriptively and
should not be interpreted as a demonstrated performance improvement.

Hit rates at or modestly below 0.5 are the expected baseline for this task
rather than a defect of the design: 20-trading-day directional forecasting from
public price history alone is approximately unpredictable under near-efficient
prices, and the abstention rule further shifts the effective base rate by
scoring only the subset of cells on which an ensemble commits to a direction.
Crucially, the agreement estimand is well-defined independent of task
difficulty: Fleiss' $\kappa$ and the $\Delta\kappa$ contrasts are functionals of
the joint distribution of agent outputs, computed without reference to outcome
labels, so the agreement results measure how similarly the agents behave
regardless of whether the task admits exploitable predictive signal. A
low-signal task is, if anything, a conservative setting for the primary
question, because agents cannot inflate measured agreement merely by all being
correct.

\subsection{Exploratory analysis of consensus errors}
\label{sec:consensus}
Average directional accuracy may conceal differences in how errors are
distributed across committee members. We therefore conduct a post hoc
exploratory analysis of consensus structure using the same frozen dataset. This
analysis was not included in the pre-registered hypotheses, and its results are
treated as hypothesis-generating rather than confirmatory. We examine three
quantities: the proportion of decisions for which all five agents select the
same label; the proportion that are unanimous, directional, and incorrect---the
``manufactured-consensus'' failure that monoculture warnings
describe~\cite{kim2025correlated}; and, conditional on an incorrect majority
decision, the proportion of agents endorsing that incorrect majority
(Table~\ref{tab:consensus}).

The unanimity rate decreases from 0.591 for HOM to 0.315 for HET-LITE and 0.267
for HET. Homogeneous ensembles therefore produce identical five-agent decisions
more than twice as frequently as fully heterogeneous ensembles. The proportion
of decisions that are unanimous, directional, and incorrect is 0.058 for HOM,
0.030 for HET-LITE, and 0.032 for HET. The homogeneous ensemble therefore
produces this form of collective error at approximately $1.84\times$ the rate of
the fully heterogeneous ensemble. The equity-clustered bootstrap gives a 95\%
confidence interval of $[1.42,\,2.52]$ for this ratio, which excludes unity.
Because this statement is inferential rather than descriptive, we also admit date
dependence under the two-way scheme used for the primary endpoint: the interval
widens to $[1.13,\,3.06]$ and still excludes unity.
HET-LITE exhibits a similar reduction in unanimous directional errors at an
approximately $2.3\times$ cost premium, compared with $4.4\times$ for HET.

These results are exploratory. Neither the configuration comparison nor the
reported statistic was pre-registered, multiple related comparisons were available
within this analysis, and no multiplicity adjustment is applied. The corresponding
intervals should therefore not be interpreted as confirmatory evidence. We do not
present HET-LITE as a recommended ensemble design. Its apparent cost efficiency is
instead treated as a hypothesis for prospective evaluation in a confirmatory
study.

Conditional on an incorrect majority vote, the proportion of agents endorsing
the incorrect majority decreases from 0.824 for HOM to 0.761 for HET-LITE and
0.636 for HET. A paired equity-clustered bootstrap over the same 100 equities,
using 2{,}000 draws and the resampling scheme of the primary endpoint, gives a
HOM$-$HET difference of $0.189$ with a 95\% confidence interval of
$[0.160,\,0.216]$. When dependence across analysis dates is also included using
the same two-way resampling procedure applied to the primary endpoint, the
interval widens to $[0.140,\,0.232]$ and remains above zero. Thus, within the
evaluated data, incorrect majority decisions in heterogeneous ensembles are
accompanied by more within-committee dissent than in the homogeneous
configuration. This result remains exploratory and does not by itself establish a
reduction in downstream decision risk.

Self-reported conviction does not exhibit a reliable positive relationship with
per-agent directional accuracy in any configuration (Fig.~\ref{fig:cal}).
Higher-conviction responses do not consistently achieve higher hit rates, and no
conviction category produces a reliably above-chance result. Consequently, the
exploratory benefit of heterogeneity appears in the structure of collective
agreement and dissent rather than in the calibration of individual agents'
reported conviction. These findings motivate a separate, appropriately powered
evaluation of calibration and collective-error risk.

\subsection{Selective prediction}
\label{subsec:selective}
Reduced agreement is only useful if it buys something downstream, so we ask
whether the configurations differ in their ability to rank their own errors.
Following the selective-classification framework of El-Yaniv and
Wiener~\cite{elyaniv2010selective}, we evaluate the risk--coverage trade-off
obtained by ranking ensemble decisions according to mean agent conviction. A
decision is the majority vote of the five agents in an (equity, date) cell on the
financial grid. Decisions whose majority is HOLD are excluded, as are cells whose
forward-return sign is undefined or zero, which leaves the directional decisions
that can be scored; the denominator for coverage is that retained set, not the
full grid. Each decision is scored as erroneous when its direction disagrees with
the realized forward-return sign, and is assigned the mean conviction its five
agents reported. Decisions are ranked by that mean conviction in descending
order, coverage is swept from the single most confident decision to the full set,
and selective risk at a coverage level is the error rate among the decisions
retained at that level. AURC is the trapezoidal area under the resulting
risk--coverage curve, so lower is better. The HOM$-$HET contrast is formed within
each bootstrap draw rather than between arms, since both configurations are
measured on the same equities, and its interval is the equity-clustered bootstrap
used for the primary endpoint, with the two-way scheme reported alongside it.

We evaluate whether reduced agreement improves selective prediction by ranking
ensemble decisions according to mean agent conviction. AURC is $0.553$ for HOM,
$0.568$ for HET-LITE, and $0.554$ for HET. The HOM$-$HET difference is $-0.001$
(95\% CI $[-0.059,\,0.060]$). The corresponding two-way interval is
$[-0.113,\,0.115]$. Both intervals contain zero.

Restricting evaluation to the most confident half of decisions does not
materially reduce selective risk in any configuration. These results therefore do
not establish an improvement in calibration or abstention quality. The agreement
and cost frontier of Section~\ref{subsec:frontier} describes the cost associated
with greater behavioral diversity, not a demonstrated improvement in decision
quality.

\subsection{Cross-domain replication: clinical knowledge}
\label{subsec:crossdomain}
To test whether the observed ordering is specific to the financial task, we
repeated the protocol using 537 questions from the MMLU professional-medicine and
clinical-knowledge subsets~\cite{hendrycks2021mmlu}. Each item has four response
options and one correct answer. Choice order is shuffled deterministically to
remove positional imbalance in the published answer key. The five-agent
structure, three configurations, seeding procedure, temperature, number of runs,
and estimators remain unchanged. The replication design, predicted direction, and
decision rule were registered before collection. Confidence intervals for the
clinical replication are obtained by resampling MMLU items with replacement,
since the equity and analysis-date clustering structure of the financial
experiment does not apply to this task.

Agreement decreases from $\kappa = 0.881$ for HOM to $0.809$ for HET, giving
$\Delta\kappa_{\mathrm{HOM-HET}} = 0.072$ (95\% CI $[0.040,\,0.104]$), compared
with $0.336$ in the financial experiment. Error correlation decreases by
$\Delta\phi_{\mathrm{HOM-HET}} = 0.393$ (95\% CI $[0.325,\,0.459]$), compared
with $0.454$ in finance. The agreement contrast therefore changes substantially
more across the two tasks than the error correlation contrast.

The pooled clinical comparison still combines provider and capability
differences. However, one same-provider pair and one cross-provider pair have
nearly identical member accuracy, $0.951$ and $0.955$ respectively. Their error
correlations are $0.954$ and $0.607$, a difference of $0.347$
(95\% CI $[0.207,\,0.507]$). The provider-associated ordering therefore persists
after approximate capability matching, although provider remains confounded with
other model-specific characteristics.

The cross-domain replication varies domain and response structure while
intentionally retaining the five-agent ensemble, prompt structure, aggregation
rule, and evaluated model mixtures. It therefore tests whether the observed
ordering is confined to the financial task, rather than establishing invariance
across ensemble-design choices. The replication supports the ordering across two
substantially different domains; it does not establish that heterogeneity
improves predictive accuracy, and the agreement contrast is substantially smaller
in the clinical arm, so the magnitude is task-dependent.

\subsection{Robustness: capability-matched control}
\label{sec:control}
The primary ensemble configurations vary both provider identity and model
capability tier. To reduce this confounding, we conduct a pre-registered
capability-matched control using three OpenAI efficient-tier agents and two
Google efficient-tier agents (Het-SameTier). An equivalent efficient-tier xAI
model was not available, so this control varies provider across two rather than
three model families. Across the same 100-equity and 12-date grid, the
homogeneous baseline produces $\kappa_{\mathrm{HOM}}=0.552$, whereas the
capability-matched heterogeneous configuration produces
$\kappa_{\mathrm{HET\text{-}SameTier}}=0.236$. The resulting contrast is
\[
\Delta\kappa_{\mathrm{HOM-SameTier}}=0.315,\quad
95\%\ \mathrm{CI}\ [0.288,\ 0.341],
\]
with a two-way interval of $[0.270,\,0.361]$; both exclude zero. This capability-matched estimate is approximately 94\% of
the primary HOM-to-HET contrast. The result indicates that the observed
reduction in agreement is largely preserved when model tier is held approximately
constant. The contrast therefore remains substantial under approximate
capability matching, although that control does not isolate provider from other
model-specific characteristics, which are not independently identified by this
design. The capability-matched control therefore reduces, but does not eliminate,
the capability-tier confound, because matching is approximate and covers only two
providers. The close reproduction of $\kappa_{\mathrm{HOM}}$ across independently
collected datasets provides an additional consistency check for the homogeneous
baseline.

\subsection{Robustness: temperature sensitivity}
Agreement can vary with sampling temperature, raising the possibility that the
primary result is specific to the main operating point of $T=0.7$. We therefore
evaluated a separate stratified subgrid comprising eight equities, two analysis
dates, three ensemble configurations, and three independent runs at
$T\in\{0.0,0.7,1.0\}$. These observations were stored separately from the frozen
confirmatory dataset and analyzed using the same seeded estimator. The ordering
$\kappa_{\mathrm{HOM}}>\kappa_{\mathrm{HET\text{-}LITE}}>\kappa_{\mathrm{HET}}$
is preserved at every tested temperature. The corresponding HOM-to-HET contrasts
are 0.299, 0.509, and 0.385 at $T=0.0$, $0.7$, and $1.0$, respectively, with
95\% confidence intervals excluding zero in each case (Table~\ref{tab:temp}).
Absolute agreement levels, however, vary non-monotonically with temperature; for
example, $\kappa_{\mathrm{HOM}}$ ranges from approximately 0.33 to 0.67. This
variation reflects changes in both within-cell concordance and the marginal
distribution of BUY, HOLD, and SELL responses. At $T=0$, the homogeneous
configuration produces a more nearly uniform label distribution, whereas at
$T=0.7$ and $T=1.0$, responses are more concentrated in HOLD. The results
therefore indicate that the relative reduction in agreement associated with
provider heterogeneity is robust within the tested subgrid, while absolute
agreement remains temperature-dependent. We note that the subgrid's contrast at
the main operating point ($0.509$ at $T=0.7$) differs from the full-grid
estimate ($0.336$) at the same temperature; this discrepancy is attributable to
the sampling variance of the small $8\times 2$ subgrid, whose wide confidence
interval ($[0.21,\ 0.78]$) comfortably contains the full-grid estimate. The
subgrid is accordingly informative about the sign and ordering of the effect,
not its magnitude. Its intervals are clustered on the eight equities only: the
subgrid spans two analysis dates, which is too few clusters to support
date-clustered or two-way inference, so this analysis contributes no independent
multiway evidence and the intervals below should be read as indicative rather
than as a test. Because this analysis uses a substantially smaller grid than
the primary experiment, it is interpreted as supporting evidence rather than an
independent replication. The temperature
analysis was conducted under a documented protocol amendment and does not alter
the frozen confirmatory dataset.

\subsection{Relation to existing ensemble methods}
This research paper does not propose a new ensemble algorithm, so a direct
performance
benchmark against existing ensemble methods is not the primary comparison.
Methods such as mixture-of-agents, multi-agent debate, and diversity-based
ensemble selection are generally evaluated using downstream accuracy or win
rate~\cite{wang2024moa}. The present study instead measures how ensemble
composition changes output agreement, error dependence, and inference cost under
fixed task conditions.

The distinction is one of estimand rather than of quality. Mixture-of-agents,
debate, pruning, and ensemble-selection studies ask whether a particular
aggregation or selection method improves downstream performance, and a
leaderboard comparison is the appropriate test of that question. This study holds
the decision task, prompt, information set, and aggregation rule fixed and asks
how ensemble \emph{composition} relates to output agreement, error dependence,
and inference cost. A leaderboard-style accuracy comparison would therefore
answer a different question than the one posed here.

The appropriate comparison with prior work is methodological, along four axes:
how diversity or agreement is measured, whether error dependence is separated
from categorical agreement, whether sampling artifacts are controlled, and
whether uncertainty and inference cost are quantified. The classifier-ensemble
literature already establishes that diversity and ensemble accuracy are related
but not interchangeable~\cite{kuncheva2003diversity}, which is why reduced
agreement is not treated here as evidence of improved accuracy. The contribution
is therefore a measurement framework that separates output agreement from error
correlation, controls the shared-seed artifact observed in repeated model
sampling, quantifies uncertainty under crossed dependence, and incorporates
inference cost. These measurements complement performance-based evaluations
rather than constitute a competing aggregation method.

\section{Discussion}
The results provide a quantitative characterization of the relationship between
provider heterogeneity, ensemble agreement, and inference cost. Within-ensemble
agreement decreases from $\kappa=0.552$ for the homogeneous configuration to
$\kappa=0.215$ for the fully heterogeneous configuration, while inference cost
increases by approximately $4.4\times$. The provider-pair analysis further shows
that agents from the same provider agree more frequently than agents from
different providers under identical prompt and information conditions. Together
with the capability-matched analysis, these findings show that the
provider-associated ordering persists across the evaluated comparisons, although
provider remains confounded with other model-specific characteristics.

Reduced agreement is a necessary but not sufficient condition for improved
ensemble performance. Greater behavioral diversity is useful only when the
additional judgments provide relevant information or enable more reliable
downstream decisions. In the present study, configurations with greater
cross-provider heterogeneity show substantially lower within-ensemble agreement
but no statistically reliable improvement in average directional accuracy. This result
is consistent with the algorithmic-monoculture
literature~\cite{kim2025correlated} in showing that greater behavioral diversity
does not automatically translate into greater correctness. The exploratory
consensus analysis nevertheless indicates that heterogeneity changes the
structure of collective errors. Homogeneous ensembles produce unanimous
incorrect directional decisions at approximately $1.84\times$ the rate of fully
heterogeneous ensembles and exhibit greater concentration of agents around an
incorrect majority decision. These findings suggest that disagreement may
provide value through reduced error concentration and increased visibility of
dissent, even when mean accuracy does not improve. However, this interpretation
remains exploratory and requires confirmatory evaluation using an appropriately
powered downstream endpoint.

The agreement--cost frontier provides a practical framework for ensemble
selection. Practitioners should not assume that the additional cost of provider
heterogeneity is justified solely because it reduces agreement. Instead, they
should evaluate whether the resulting diversity improves a task-relevant
objective, such as calibration, robustness, abstention quality, or the frequency
of concentrated collective errors. In the exploratory analysis, HET-LITE
achieves a reduction in unanimous directional errors comparable to HET at an
approximately $2.3\times$ cost premium, compared with $4.4\times$ for HET. Because
this pattern was identified post hoc, it should be interpreted as
hypothesis-generating rather than as deployment guidance. It motivates
prospective evaluation of HET-LITE in a confirmatory design specifically powered
to assess the tradeoff between collective error structure and inference cost.

The shared-seed pilot also has a methodological implication for empirical
studies of LLM ensembles. When repeated model instances receive the same seed or
otherwise share elements of the sampling trajectory, their outputs may not
represent independent draws, causing within-ensemble agreement and configuration
contrasts to be materially distorted. Agreement studies should therefore
document the sampling context, assign separate reproducible seeds to individual
agents when supported, and include a diagnostic for residual within-run
dependence.

\section{Threats to Validity}
\textbf{Heterogeneity vs.\ capability confound.}
The primary ensemble configurations differ in both provider composition and
model capability tier. HOM contains five instances of an efficient-tier OpenAI
model, whereas the heterogeneous configurations introduce models from other
providers, including higher-tier models. Consequently, part of the observed
reduction in agreement could reflect capability differences rather than provider
identity alone. Two analyses reduce, but do not eliminate, this concern. First,
the provider-pair matrix (Fig.~\ref{fig:provider}) compares agents evaluating
the same equity-date cell under identical prompt and information conditions and
shows substantially higher agreement for same-provider pairs ($0.80$--$0.92$)
than for cross-provider pairs ($0.43$--$0.63$). Second, the capability-matched
control (Section~\ref{sec:control}) holds model tier approximately constant
across OpenAI and Google and produces a contrast of $\Delta\kappa=0.315$,
approximately 94\% of the primary HOM-to-HET effect. The reduction in agreement
therefore remains substantial after approximate tier matching. However, the
control covers only two providers because an equivalent efficient-tier xAI model
was unavailable, and differences in training data, architecture, alignment
procedures, and effective capability cannot be completely separated from
provider identity.

\textbf{Scope and generalizability.}
The primary experiment evaluates a directional financial-analysis task with a
three-class BUY, HOLD, and SELL output space, while
Section~\ref{subsec:crossdomain} provides a pre-registered replication on 537
expert clinical-decision items with a four-option response structure. The ordering
reproduces across these two substantially different tasks, indicating that the
result is not confined to the original financial setting. Its magnitude, however,
differs materially across domains, with the agreement contrast in the clinical
replication approximately one fifth of that observed in finance.

Several design dimensions remain fixed across the study, including prompt format,
five-agent ensemble size, majority-vote aggregation, and the particular model and
provider mixtures evaluated. Other task classes, model combinations, ensemble
sizes, prompting strategies, or aggregation rules may produce different levels of
agreement, error dependence, and cost. The present results therefore support
replication of the observed ordering across the two tested domains without
implying that its magnitude is invariant across settings.

\textbf{Model-version pinning.}
Proprietary model endpoints may be updated by their providers without preserving
identical behavior under the same product name. We mitigate this limitation by
recording the exact model identifiers, timestamps, seeds, prompt hashes, and raw
responses associated with every call and by treating the frozen, hash-verified
dataset as the empirical object of record. The findings therefore apply to the
archived outputs generated during the reported collection period and may not
reproduce exactly with later endpoint versions.

\textbf{Single-run collection.}
Although the design contains three independent runs per configuration, the
confirmatory responses were collected within a single experimental period.
Provider-side updates, transient service conditions, or undocumented inference
changes occurring outside that period are therefore not averaged across multiple
collection windows. Repeating the experiment at later dates would be necessary
to evaluate temporal stability.

\textbf{Accuracy underpowered.}
The experiment was designed and powered for the agreement endpoint rather than
for differences in directional accuracy. The accuracy analysis uses a
descriptive proxy based on the sign of the 20-trading-day forward return, treats
HOLD as an abstention, and produces relatively wide confidence intervals. It
therefore cannot establish that heterogeneity improves or fails to improve
predictive performance. The reported hit rates should not be interpreted as
evidence of investment alpha, trading profitability, or economically significant
forecasting ability.

\section{Conclusion}
This study presents a pre-registered empirical evaluation of how agreement and
inference cost differ across LLM ensemble configurations of increasing
cross-provider heterogeneity. Across a
54{,}000-call primary experiment, within-ensemble agreement decreases
monotonically as provider diversity increases, with a primary HOM-to-HET
contrast of $\Delta\kappa=0.336$ (95\% CI: $[0.304, 0.369]$). This reduction is
accompanied by an approximately $4.4\times$ increase in inference cost,
producing an explicit agreement--cost frontier for the evaluated
configurations. A capability-matched control preserves approximately 94\% of the
primary contrast, which therefore remains substantial under approximate
capability matching; provider nevertheless remains bundled with other
model-specific characteristics and is not independently identified. We
also introduce an independent-draw measurement protocol that controls dependence
caused by shared sampling seeds. In a controlled pilot, shared per-run seeding
inflated the estimated agreement contrast by approximately fourfold,
demonstrating the importance of checking for residual within-run dependence and
avoiding shared-seed artifacts in
ensemble-agreement studies.

Cross-provider heterogeneity does not produce a statistically reliable
improvement in average directional accuracy at the scale of this experiment.
However, exploratory results indicate that homogeneous ensembles generate
unanimous incorrect directional decisions at approximately $1.84\times$ the rate
of fully heterogeneous ensembles and concentrate more agents around incorrect
majority decisions. These findings suggest that heterogeneity may provide value
by reducing concentrated collective errors and preserving disagreement signals,
although this interpretation requires confirmatory evaluation. Overall, the
results show that model diversity can reduce output agreement and error
correlation, but the practical value of that reduced agreement
must be established against a downstream objective such as calibration,
robustness, abstention quality, or collective-error reduction. Future work
should evaluate the observed agreement ordering across additional tasks,
ensemble compositions, and fully tier-matched provider configurations.
