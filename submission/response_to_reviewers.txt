Reviewer #1, Concern #1

Reviewer comment:

Reduced agreement is not shown to improve accuracy or any decision-relevant outcome. Link the
agreement-cost frontier to calibration, abstention, robustness, or tail-error reduction.

Author response:

  - We agree, and we have not claimed otherwise. The manuscript reports that heterogeneity
    produces no reliable gain in average directional accuracy; the reviewer is right that this
    leaves the frontier without a demonstrated downstream benefit.
  - We now test the most directly relevant of the four suggested outcomes, abstention, using a
    selective-prediction analysis, and we report the result even though it is null.
  - Ensemble decisions are ranked by mean agent conviction, coverage is swept from the most
    confident decision to the full set, and selective risk is the error rate among retained
    decisions. The area under the risk-coverage curve is 0.553 for HOM, 0.568 for HET-LITE and
    0.554 for HET, against full-coverage risks of 0.562, 0.537 and 0.512. Restricting to the most
    confident half of decisions does not materially reduce risk in any configuration.
  - Conviction as elicited here does not rank errors usefully, so we claim no calibration or
    abstention benefit.
  - We do report one decision-relevant quantity that does move: the rate at which an ensemble is
    unanimous and wrong. See our response to Concern #4.

Author action:

  - Added "Selective prediction" to the results, reporting the risk-coverage analysis and AURC
    for all three configurations.
  - The frontier is now stated explicitly as a description of what diversity costs, not as
    evidence that it improves decision quality.

Findings come from one financial task, one prompt, three labels, and fixed five-agent ensembles.

Author response:

  - We have run the replication the reviewer asks for, on a domain chosen to be as unlike the
    original as possible: expert clinical decision-making, using the MMLU professional-medicine
    and clinical-knowledge subsets, 537 four-option items under the MIT licence with no personal
    data.
  - The protocol is otherwise unchanged: the same five agents, the same three configurations, the

    three.
  - We have not varied ensemble size or aggregation rule. Those are single-parameter sweeps on the

Reviewer #2, Concern #2

Reviewer comment:

No clear explanation about fair comparison with current state-of-the-art approach.

Author response:

  - The manuscript did not explain why it contains no head-to-head benchmark, and that was a gap.
  - This article does not propose an ensemble method. Methods such as mixture-of-agents,
    multi-agent debate and diversity-maximising ensemble selection are evaluated on task
    performance, and the comparison that matters for them is accuracy against a baseline. The
    object of study here is different: how much behavioral de-correlation a given ensemble
    composition buys and what it costs, holding task, prompt and information set fixed.
  - The fair comparison is therefore one of measurement. Prior work reporting ensemble diversity
    typically uses a single agreement index on pooled outputs, without separating agreement from
    error correlation, without an independent-draw protocol, and without interval estimates. The
    manuscript now shows that the first distinction changes the interpretation, the second changes
    the estimate roughly fourfold in our pilot, and the third determines whether a reported
    difference is resolvable at all.

Author action:

  - Added "Relation to state-of-the-art ensemble methods".

Reviewer #3, Concern #2

Reviewer comment:

Recompute uncertainty while accounting for both repeated observations within equities and shared
analysis dates/common market conditions. Justify the bootstrap unit formally, evaluate two-way
equity/date clustering or an appropriate multiway resampling strategy, and regenerate all main and
exploratory confidence intervals accordingly.

Author response:

  - We have done this, and the primary result survives it.
  - The equity is the pre-registered unit because it is the level at which repeated observations
    are nested: each security is evaluated on twelve dates and shares company-specific context. We
    now state that justification rather than assuming it.
  - Dates are a second, crossed source of dependence. We report the primary contrast under three
    schemes: equity clustering (100 clusters), date clustering (12 clusters), and a two-way
    scheme in which equities and dates are resampled independently.
  - The point estimate is 0.3363 in all three. The intervals are [0.3035, 0.3689] for equities,
    [0.2989, 0.3656] for dates and [0.2786, 0.3877] two-way. The two-way interval is the widest, as
    expected when two crossed dependence structures are admitted at once, and all three exclude
    zero.
  - The reviewer is right that the earlier equity interval, [0.3131, 0.3628], contradicted the
    primary [0.3035, 0.3689] reported in the abstract, Table 2, the Results and the Conclusion.
    They were presented as the same estimand and should have agreed exactly. They did not because
    the two were computed by different procedures: the primary bootstrap resamples equities with
    replacement and counts an equity drawn twice as two clusters, while the robustness table
    treated each draw as a set, so a repeated draw counted once and every draw used only the
    roughly 63 distinct equities of a 100-draw multiset. That is subsampling, not a cluster
    bootstrap, and it understated the variance. The robustness table now honours multiplicity and
    uses the pre-registered equity ordering, so its equity row reproduces the primary interval
    exactly; the code asserts that equality on every run and fails if it is ever broken. The date
    and two-way intervals widen for the same reason. No point estimate changed and every interval
    still excludes zero.
  - We note that the date margin has only twelve clusters, a regime in which cluster-robust
    inference is known to under-cover. The equity-clustered interval therefore remains the
    pre-registered primary and the other two are reported as sensitivity analyses.
  - On scope, the reviewer asked that all main and exploratory intervals account for equity and
    date dependence. The two-way scheme is applied to the primary endpoint and, in this revision,
    to the two exploratory contrasts that carry an inferential claim: the pile-on contrast widens
    from [0.160, 0.216] to [0.140, 0.232] and still excludes zero, and the AURC contrast widens
    from [-0.059, 0.060] to [-0.113, 0.115] and still contains zero. Neither conclusion changes.
    The remaining exploratory quantities are reported as descriptive statistics without an
    inferential claim attached, and retain the pre-registered paired equity-clustered bootstrap;
    they are interpreted as exploratory throughout and we do not draw conclusions from them that
    a wider interval could overturn.

Reviewer #3, Concern #3

Reviewer comment:

Fleiss' kappa measures agreement, not correlation. Either remove "de-correlation"/"less-correlated
judgments" throughout the title, abstract, figures, discussion, and conclusion, or add actual
dependence/error-correlation analyses that justify those terms. Explicitly distinguish output
agreement, error correlation, sampling independence, model diversity, and provider diversity.

Author response:

  - The reviewer is right that kappa measures agreement irrespective of correctness, and the
    original manuscript acknowledged the distinction without measuring the second quantity. We
    have taken the second option and added the analysis.
  - For every pair of agents within a cell we compute the Pearson correlation between their binary
    error indicators against the forward-return sign. Error correlation falls monotonically: 0.984
    for HOM, 0.742 for HET-LITE and 0.530 for HET, giving 0.454 (95% CI [0.376, 0.534]).
  - Because abstentions are excluded, the scored subset differs across configurations. We repeat
    the calculation counting HOLD as an error, which fixes an identical subset for every
    configuration: the contrast is then 0.309 (95% CI [0.262, 0.354]). Both treatments give a
    monotone decline whose interval excludes zero, so the estimate is bracketed rather than
    dependent on how abstentions are handled.
  - Mean per-agent error rates are close across configurations, so the decline is a change in
    dependence and not a consequence of differing accuracy.
  - We also state that de-correlation is partial rather than complete: heterogeneous agents still
    err together more often than independence would imply.
  - We have added the five-way distinction the reviewer asks for, and we now define the term
    explicitly as sense (2), error correlation, with the phi contrast rather than the kappa
    contrast as its evidence.

Author action:

  - Added "Agreement, error correlation, and decorrelation", containing the five-way distinction
    and the phi analysis under both label treatments. That subsection defines decorrelation as a
    reduction in error correlation measured by phi, and the sections that report kappa have been
    retitled accordingly: the primary results section is now "Heterogeneity reduces ensemble
    agreement".
  - The abstract now reports the phi contrast alongside the kappa contrast.
